Business Systems Design & Analysis
A reference guide, September 2026

Designing business systems when the method is no longer one method

Waterfall gave us gates and traceability. Agile gave us feedback loops and empirical planning. AI-assisted delivery is now forcing a third shape: specification-driven, verification-first, human-in-the-loop. This guide compiles current practice and walks the analysis-and-design process end to end, from user research to architectural tradeoffs.

Choose a solution type

Three lineages, one delivery reality

Almost no enterprise runs a pure method today. Research and practitioner reports converge on a dual-track model: sprint-based execution at the team level under milestone reviews and stage gates at the program and portfolio level. The names differ, the shape is consistent.

Plan-driven (Waterfall, V-model)

Sequential phases with formal exit criteria. Requirements are baselined, then designed, built, verified, and validated. Strong on traceability, regulatory evidence, and physical or contractual constraints.

  • Best when requirements are stable and mistakes are expensive
  • Weak when the problem itself is uncertain
  • Testing mirrors each abstraction level (V-model)
V-model · Stage-Gate · PRINCE2 · ISO/IEC/IEEE 15288

Empirical (Agile, Lean)

Short cycles, working increments, and continuous re-planning from evidence. Requirements emerge through discovery and are refined as understanding grows. Strong on learning speed and stakeholder alignment.

  • Best when value is discovered, not specified
  • Weak on cross-team dependencies and audit trails at scale
  • Scaled frameworks re-introduce cadences and gates
Scrum · Kanban · SAFe · Disciplined Agile · Dual-track discovery

AI-native (spec-driven, verification-first)

AI agents act as participants in the lifecycle, not tools bolted onto it. Humans author intent as detailed specifications (domain model, business rules, constraints); agents implement; humans and automated checks verify continuously. Looks like Waterfall's upfront rigor with Agile's iterative validation.

  • Best when execution is cheap and correctness must be proven
  • Weak when intent is fuzzy: garbage spec, confident garbage output
  • Governance shifts from "did we build it" to "can we prove it does what we said"
AI-DLC (AWS, 2025) · Spec-Driven Development · ISO/IEC 42001 · EU AI Act evidence trails

Reference

Working definitions used throughout this guide. Where a term has a formal home (BABOK, ISO 15288, INCOSE), the sense here is the practitioner's, not the standard's.

Acceptance criteria
The observable conditions under which a requirement is considered met. Written before design so verification is not negotiated after the fact.
Architecture decision record (ADR)
A short document capturing one significant decision: context, options considered, decision, and consequences. The decision log is the sum of ADRs.
As-is / to-be
The current state of a process as it actually runs, versus the designed future state. The gap between them is the scope of the work.
Baseline
An agreed, versioned snapshot of an artifact (requirements, KPIs, architecture) against which change is measured and controlled.
Business rule
A statement that defines or constrains a decision: condition, outcome, exceptions, and owner. Rules belong to the business and should be catalogued separately from the code that enforces them.
Decision point
A step in a process where a choice is made. Phase 4 assigns each one to a rule, a model, or a person.
Deterministic system
A solution whose output is fully determined by its inputs and explicit rules; the same input always yields the same result, and the reason can be traced.
Dual-track
Running discovery (research, analysis) and delivery (build, release) as parallel, continuous streams rather than sequential phases.
Evidence pack
The collected proof that a solution meets its specification and controls: test results, evaluation results, pilot outcomes, sign-offs. In regulated settings, what the auditor reads.
Exit criteria / stage gate
The conditions a phase must satisfy before the next begins. Gates are where plan-driven governance lives; empirical methods replace them with definition-of-done and increment reviews.
Human-in-the-loop (HITL)
A design in which a person reviews, approves, or overrides an automated or model-generated decision before it takes effect.
Human-centered process
A solution expressed as roles, procedures, decision rights, and tools for people, rather than as software. Often the cheapest fix and the most neglected.
Non-functional requirement (NFR)
A quality the system must exhibit rather than a function it performs: throughput, latency, availability, auditability, explainability, security.
Probabilistic / AI component
A solution element whose output is a prediction or generation with an associated confidence; the same input may yield different results and the reason is often not fully traceable.
Process mining
Reconstructing an as-is process model from system event logs. Reveals the real flow, including rework and workarounds, but only what the system recorded.
Requirements traceability
The ability to follow each requirement backward to its source need and forward to the design element and verification that satisfies it.
Solution allocation
The mapping of each decision point or capability in the to-be process to a solution type: deterministic, AI, or human.
Specification-driven development (SDD)
A lifecycle in which a detailed, versioned specification is the ground truth from which implementation (often AI-generated) is produced and against which it is continuously verified.
Value stream
The end-to-end sequence of steps that delivers a result a customer values, measured in touch time versus wait time.
Verification vs. validation
Verification asks whether the system does what the specification says. Validation asks whether it solves the problem the stakeholders have. Both are required; they fail independently.

How to run the six-phase spine on a real engagement. A playbook is about judgment: what to do, in what order, and what to watch for. The runbook is about execution.

Before you start

  • Name the sponsor who can say no. If nobody can stop the project, nobody can be accountable for it either.
  • Pick the governance posture (plan-driven, hybrid, empirical, AI-native) explicitly and write it down. Most failures are an unspoken mismatch between what governance expects and how the team works.
  • Agree the baseline KPI you are trying to move before anyone proposes a solution.

Phase by phase

1
Discover. Sample every role that touches the workflow, including the ones who work around it. Watch the work before asking about it. Stop when new interviews stop producing new themes, typically 8 to 15 sessions per role cluster.
2
Analyze. Model the as-is with measured times, not estimates. Quantify the cost of the current state. Run root-cause on the top three frictions and confirm at least one with data. Draw the scope boundary and name what is out.
3
Specify. Write requirements as capabilities with acceptance measures. Pull business rules into a catalogue with owners. Write NFRs and controls with numbers. Build the traceability matrix as you go, not at the end. In AI-native mode, this is the deliverable; spend the time here.
4
Design. Draft the to-be process, then allocate every decision point to a rule, a model, or a person using the solution-type chooser as a prompt. Prototype the riskiest path first and walk it with operators. Check incentives: who gets more work and did you ask them.
5
Architect. Map dependencies across people, process, data, systems, AI, integrations, controls, vendors. Write ADRs for the five tradeoffs that matter. Design controls to requirements. Run a failure-mode pass: what breaks, who notices, how it degrades.
6
Validate. Verify against the spec, validate against the baseline KPI with a pilot or before-after. Get operators trained and consulted before go-live. Stand up benefits tracking with owners and dates. Hold the retrospective on the analysis itself.

Anti-patterns to watch

  • Solution-first. The request arrives as "we need a tool for X." Route it back through Phases 1 and 2 anyway; the tool is a hypothesis.
  • Requester-as-user. The sponsor's story is treated as the operation's reality. Shadow the operation.
  • Automation by default. Every decision point is automated because it can be. Use error cost and explainability from the chooser to set the threshold.
  • Spec by committee, verification by nobody. Especially in AI-native delivery: a detailed spec that no one owns and no check enforces produces confident, wrong output at speed.
  • Gate theatre. Reviews that approve everything are not gates. Publish the exit criteria and record what was waived.

Repeatable procedures for the operational moments of an engagement. Each one is meant to be followed as written, then improved through the retrospective.

R1 · Engagement kickoff (week 0)

  1. Confirm sponsor, decision rights, and escalation path in writing.
  2. Record the governance posture and which gates are live (use the blend control on the main page and copy the caption).
  3. Create the artifact register: one entry per expected artifact per phase, with owner and status.
  4. Set up the requirements traceability matrix template and the ADR folder.
  5. Schedule gate reviews on the calendar now, with named attendees.
  6. Publish the stakeholder map and the research sampling plan.

R2 · Weekly rhythm

  • Monday: phase stand-up. What artifact moves this week, what blocks it, who is needed.
  • Midweek: working sessions with process and data owners. Every session ends with a change to a named artifact or a recorded decision.
  • Friday: artifact register update; risk and dependency register review; sponsor note (five lines: progress, decisions needed, risks, next week, help wanted).

R3 · Gate review procedure

  1. Circulate the phase's exit criteria and the artifacts 48 hours ahead.
  2. Reviewer checks each criterion: met, not met, or waived. Waivers require a named owner and a date.
  3. Decision recorded as proceed, proceed with conditions, or return. Conditions become register entries.
  4. If returned, log the reason category: research gap, unquantified problem, untestable requirement, missing owner, unresolved dependency. Track categories across engagements.

R4 · Requirements intake

  1. Capture the ask in the requester's words.
  2. Trace it to a stakeholder need and a Phase 2 finding; if none exists, route to discovery.
  3. Rewrite as a capability statement with an acceptance measure.
  4. Extract any embedded business rule into the catalogue with an owner.
  5. Assign priority and verification method; add to the matrix.

R5 · Solution-type allocation

  1. List every decision point in the to-be process.
  2. For each, answer the six chooser questions and record the leading fit.
  3. For any AI allocation, define: task, input/output schema, confidence threshold, fallback path, escalation owner, evaluation set, monitoring metric.
  4. For any human allocation, define: role, procedure, checklist, training, and the incentive check.
  5. Review allocations with operators before design proceeds.

R6 · Architecture decision

  1. Open an ADR: title, status, date, context.
  2. List at least two real options with consequences for each.
  3. Decide; record who decided and what tradeoff was accepted.
  4. Update the dependency register and the control matrix if affected.
  5. Link the ADR from the requirements it serves.

R7 · Validation and go-live

  1. Run verification against every acceptance measure; record results in the evidence pack.
  2. Run the pilot or before-after against the baseline KPI for the agreed window.
  3. Confirm operator training completion and support model.
  4. Confirm monitoring is live (including model drift and fallback rates where applicable).
  5. Go / no-go with the sponsor; record waivers.
  6. Benefits review at 30, 90, and 180 days; feed findings to the next retrospective.
